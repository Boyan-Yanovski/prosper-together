Part 1: Prosper TogetherA Tale of Specialization, Coordination and Cooperation

Free markets are often considered to be the best solution for resource allocation and utilization. They are also often seen as the natural state of things or simply as the lack of regulation or state involvement apart from enforcing individual property rights. This game series aims to illustrate that free markets cannot be universally recommended, because what decentralized decisions lead
to depends entirely on the underlying economic environment.

This game, in particular, aims to capture one core aspect of economic reality: Individuals possess unique qualities that make specialization beneficial to the whole. But specialization is risky: whoever specializes depends on the whole to provide necessities. The game can be considered an adaptation of the Jean-Jacques Rousseau's (1712-1778) "Stag hunt" game theoretical setup.

The other four are not scripted. Each carries its own appetite for risk and its own trust — in the market, and in you — and each reprices participation every turn. The only argument you can make to them is a pledge: goods from your own production, promised in exchange for the energy they invest.

Two outcomes hold once reached: everybody stays home, or everybody commits. The second is worth far more to all five. Nothing in the rules pushes the table towards it.

Direct interaction with the other players

The second usage of your reserve of goods is that it allows you to pay other players for a pledge that they will invest 1  to produce their special input. That way you can try to get collaboration going and move society toward the prosperous equilibrium. How much a player demands for a pledge depends on what the player expects the others would contribute and on the gain they expect to have if they join.

Behavior of the other players

The game has two Nash equilibria (autarky and full specialization). Autarky is the mutual best response to autarky and full specialization is the mutual best response to full specialization. So the players try to learn which equilibrium is more likely to establish itself. What the players have learned from the past behavior of other players (who contributed their special input, how many times and how much) modulated through their individual learning rates, as well as their survival considerations determine the asking  price for pledges to invest  in the special input.

After production: consume or save

After production, decide how much of your available goods (the  value of new goods + current reserve) to consume and how much to keep as a reserve.

Consume: each good adds 1 wellbeing and counts toward survival. Save: durable goods for later consumption or upfront pledges.

Survival 

One usage for reserves is to be able to consume later. The game has a health mechanic. In order to survive you have to consume at least 6  worth of goods over every 2 consecutive turns. If available goods cannot cover this turn's remaining requirement, it is Game Over. How a larger society impacts the chances for success of a lone instigator:

The game is harder with more players because there are more essential/unique trades. You now need 4 players to go to the table to beat autarky. To compensate for that you now start with 1 additional good in reserve and your survival window is now 3 turns.

The easy way

Found an institution for centralized coordination (a state) by means of a democratic vote.

The problem

Every player is better off if everyone practices their own trade instead of producing in autarky. Yet autarky is a Nash-equilibrium of market-based/decentralized coordination. The expectation that no one would contribute their special input to the table leads to actions that confirm that expectation.

On the ballot

Found the Commonwealth1

Its one law: whoever puts into producing their special input gets at least 1.01 for it. When not enough players practice their own trade such that the table yields less than 1.01 /, the Commonwealth compensates the difference by levying a tax on everyone’s production that turn.

Why everyone votes yes

  1. Trying beats staying home, whatever the others do. On the table each is topped up to 1.01 . At home it makes 1, minus the tax (if not enough players practice their trade).
  2. Everyone sees the same thing, so everyone tries.
  3. With everyone at the table, each makes 3.5 , far above 1.01. Nobody needs topping up, so nobody is taxed. The guarantee never has to be paid.

Why the vote fails now

With only 2 players left, even a full table makes less than autarky. The top-ups would come out of the same players’ production every turn, so no one votes for it.

Why 1.01 and not 1? At exactly 1, trying alone only ties with staying home, so everyone staying home would still hold. The extra 0.01 makes trying strictly better.

    Each player learns separately how much others contribute. Initial participation chance sets their optimism. Learning rate sets the weight of each new observation and stays constant: older evidence gradually fades.
